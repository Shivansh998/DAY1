Agile Methodology and Scrum — Training Notes
1. Agile Methodology — Terms and Meaning
1. Agile
Agile is a software development approach in which a team develops software in small parts instead of completing the entire project at once. The team tests the software regularly, collects feedback, and improves it. Agile also helps teams handle changing requirements.

How it works:
- Understand what the customer needs.
- Divide the project into smaller features.
- Develop and test features step by step.
- Take feedback and improve the product.
Example: A team building a shopping application develops login first, then product search, shopping cart, and payment.
2. Scrum
Scrum is a framework used to manage work using Agile principles. It organizes work into fixed-length Sprints and defines accountabilities, events, and artifacts to help the team collaborate and track progress.

How it works:
- The Product Owner prioritizes the work.
- The team plans a Sprint.
- Developers build and test the selected features.
- The team reviews results and identifies improvements.
Example: A team plans to complete the login and registration features during a two-week Sprint.
3. Sprint
A Sprint is a fixed period of one month or less in which the Scrum Team works toward a Sprint Goal. Many teams use one- or two-week Sprints. Each Sprint aims to produce a usable product increment.

How it works:
- Set a Sprint Goal.
- Select work that can reasonably be completed.
- Develop and test the selected work.
- Review the outcome and improve the process.
Example: During a two-week Sprint, the team develops and tests the registration feature.
4. Iteration
An iteration is a repeated cycle of developing, testing, and improving a product. The team uses what it learns in one cycle to improve the next. In Scrum, the Sprint is the fixed-length cycle.

How it works:
- Select a feature or improvement.
- Develop the feature.
- Test it and collect feedback.
- Make improvements in the next cycle.
Example: The team develops a login page, tests it, fixes errors, and tests it again.
5. User Story
A User Story describes a requirement from the user's point of view. It explains who needs a feature, what they need, and why it is useful.

Common format: “As a user, I want to reset my password so that I can access my account if I forget it.”

How it works:
- Understand the user's need.
- Write the requirement as a User Story.
- Define Acceptance Criteria.
- Estimate and prioritize the story.
6. Epic
An Epic is a large feature or requirement that can be divided into smaller User Stories. It may take several Sprints to complete. Breaking an Epic into smaller stories makes the work easier to understand and plan.

Example: An online shopping application's account-management Epic could contain user registration, user login, password reset, and profile management.
7. Story Point
A Story Point is a unit used to estimate the relative effort required to complete a User Story. The team considers complexity, effort, and uncertainty. Story Points are not directly equal to hours or days.

How it works:
- Discuss the requirements.
- Compare the story with previously estimated work.
- Estimate its relative effort.
- Use the estimate to help plan Sprint work.
Example: A simple feature might receive 2 points, while a more complex feature might receive 8 points. The team's agreed estimation scale determines the values.
8. Acceptance Criteria
Acceptance Criteria are the specific conditions a feature must satisfy to meet its requirements. They help developers understand what to build and testers understand what to verify.

Example:
- A user can log in with valid credentials.
- Invalid credentials are rejected.
- An appropriate error message is displayed.
- The user can access the account only after successful authentication.
2. Scrum Roles — Responsibilities
1. Product Owner (PO)
The Product Owner is accountable for maximizing the value of the product. They manage the Product Backlog, prioritize work, and help ensure that requirements are understood.

Responsibilities:
- Understand customer and business needs.
- Create and prioritize Product Backlog items.
- Clarify requirements and expected outcomes.
- Review feedback and adjust priorities.
Example: The Product Owner decides that login should be developed before the profile page because it is more important to users.
2. Scrum Master (SM)
The Scrum Master helps the team understand and use Scrum effectively. They coach the team, support useful Scrum events, and help address obstacles that affect progress.

Responsibilities:
- Help the team understand Scrum.
- Support effective Sprint Planning and other events.
- Help address obstacles.
- Encourage teamwork and continuous improvement.
Example: If a team cannot begin testing because the test environment is unavailable, the Scrum Master helps the team address the obstacle.
3. Developers
Developers are the people who create the product increment. Depending on the project, they may include programmers, testers, designers, and other specialists. They organize their work and collaborate to achieve the Sprint Goal.

Responsibilities:
- Develop the required features.
- Test the software and fix defects.
- Coordinate work with other team members.
- Ensure the product meets the Definition of Done.
Example: Developers build the login feature, test its behavior, fix errors, and prepare it for release.
4. Stakeholder
A stakeholder is a person or group interested in the product. Stakeholders can include customers, managers, business teams, and end users. They provide business context and feedback that help guide future development.

Responsibilities:
- Explain business requirements.
- Share user expectations.
- Provide feedback on the product.
- Help identify future needs and improvements.
Example: A customer reviews a new feature and suggests making the login process simpler.
3. Scrum Events and Terms — Purpose
1. Sprint Planning
Sprint Planning takes place at the beginning of a Sprint. The Scrum Team discusses why the Sprint is valuable, what work can be completed, and how the work will be done. The outcome includes a Sprint Goal and a Sprint Backlog.

How planning happens:
1. Review the prioritized Product Backlog.
2. Discuss requirements, dependencies, and team capacity.
3. Select suitable work for the Sprint.
4. Define the Sprint Goal and plan the work.
Example: The team selects login and registration features for the next two weeks.
2. Daily Scrum
The Daily Scrum is a 15-minute event for Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan. It helps them coordinate work and identify problems early.

What happens:
- Check progress toward the Sprint Goal.
- Discuss work that remains.
- Identify obstacles or dependencies.
- Adjust the plan for the next day's work.
Example: A developer reports that login development is complete, but testing is blocked by a defect. The team coordinates how to address it.
3. Sprint Review
The Sprint Review takes place at the end of the Sprint. The Scrum Team and stakeholders inspect the product increment and discuss feedback, changes, and future priorities.

What happens:
- Demonstrate the completed product increment.
- Discuss the results and important findings.
- Collect stakeholder feedback.
- Consider what work should be prioritized next.
Example: The team demonstrates the login feature and receives feedback about improving error messages.
4. Sprint Retrospective
The Sprint Retrospective focuses on improving the team's quality and effectiveness. Team members discuss how they worked during the Sprint and agree on practical improvements.

What happens:
- Discuss what went well.
- Identify problems and their causes.
- Suggest improvements.
- Agree on actions for the next Sprint.
Example: The team realizes that testing started too late and decides to involve testers earlier in the next Sprint.
5. Sprint Backlog
The Sprint Backlog is the Developers' plan for the current Sprint. It includes the Sprint Goal, selected Product Backlog items, and the plan for delivering them. It can change as the team learns more during the Sprint.

What it contains:
- The Sprint Goal.
- Selected Product Backlog items.
- Tasks or activities needed to complete the work.
- The current plan for achieving the goal.
Example: The Sprint Backlog includes tasks for developing login, writing tests, fixing defects, and documenting the results.
6. Sprint Goal
The Sprint Goal is the single objective that gives the Sprint its direction. It helps the team make decisions and coordinate work, even when individual tasks change.

How it works:
- Identify the most valuable outcome for the Sprint.
- Agree on the goal during Sprint Planning.
- Use the goal to guide decisions during the Sprint.
- Review progress toward achieving it.
Example: “Deliver a working user login feature with basic validation and error handling.”
4. Definition of Done (DoD)
The Definition of Done is a shared description of the quality requirements that must be met for a product increment to be considered complete. It ensures that everyone understands what “done” means.

How it works:
- The team follows agreed quality standards.
- Required development and testing are completed.
- Necessary documentation and checks are completed.
- Work is considered Done only when it meets the agreed requirements.
Example: A team may require code completion, successful tests, defect checks, and updated documentation before a feature is considered Done.

Acceptance Criteria vs Definition of Done: Acceptance Criteria describe what a particular feature must do, while the Definition of Done describes the overall quality standard for completed work.
5. How a Scrum Team Plans Work
Imagine a team developing an online shopping application.
1. Product Backlog: The Product Owner lists requirements such as login, registration, product search, cart, and payment.
2. Break down requirements: A large Epic, such as account management, is divided into User Stories with Acceptance Criteria.
3. Estimate work: Developers estimate Story Points to compare the effort of different stories.
4. Sprint Planning: The team selects work, agrees on the Sprint Goal, and creates the Sprint Backlog.
5. Develop and test: Developers build features, coordinate during Daily Scrum, and check that work meets the Definition of Done.
6. Sprint Review: The team demonstrates the product increment and gathers feedback.
7. Sprint Retrospective: The team discusses improvements for the next Sprint.
8. Next Sprint: The team starts the next cycle with updated priorities.
